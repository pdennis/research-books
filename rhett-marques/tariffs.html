Tariffs Negatively Impacted Alabama

Trump’s Tariffs Weakened Alabama’s Automobile And Aircraft Industry

Because Of Trump’s Tariffs, The “Pillars Of Alabama’s Manufacturing Economy,” Car And Aircraft Exports, Dropped By $226 Million. According to the Montgomery Advertiser, "When President Donald Trump announced sweeping tariffs on imported steel and aluminum in early 2025, Alabama manufacturers braced for higher costs and supply chain disruptions. More than a year later, the state’s automotive and aerospace sectors have had time to adjust, providing a clearer picture of who benefited, who faced challenges, and what the tariffs meant for Alabama’s economy. While the state remains a manufacturing powerhouse led by its automotive industry, exports fell nearly 6% year over year while imports jumped more than 20%, according to data from the Observatory of Economic Complexity. […] Alabama’s largest export industries recorded notable declines. Car exports fell by $187 million compared with April 2025, while aircraft exports dropped by $39 million. The decrease occurred in two sectors that state leaders frequently cite as pillars of Alabama’s manufacturing economy." [Montgomery Advertiser, 7/16/26]

Tariffs Hurt Alabama Farmers

An Alabama Soybean Farmer Said Trump’s Tariffs Have Caused Prices To Drop “Below The Cost Of Production” For Farmers Who Were “Already Dealing With Tighter Margins.” According to WBRC, "Some Alabama farmers say they’re already feeling the sting of President Donald Trump’s newly raised tariffs on Chinese imports, now increased to 125%.  Jeremy Wilson, a soybean farmer and member of the Alabama Soybean and Corn Association, said farm income is already down 25% since 2022, and the tariffs are only compounding the strain. ‘Soybeans brought in over $24 billion last year,’ Wilson said. ‘But prices have now dropped below the cost of production. We can’t forward market. We can’t borrow. And we’re paying more for fertilizer and chemicals.’ Wilson said soybeans, the U.S.’s most exported crop, are particularly vulnerable to trade disruptions. He added that while consumers may not yet feel the effects, farmers are already dealing with tighter margins and limited financing options." [WBRC, 4/16/25]

An Alabama Farmer Said High Tariffs Would Force Him To Either “Increase Prices Or Sell At A Loss.” According to WAFF, "North Alabama farmers said they are concerned about the impacts that President Donald Trump’s recent tariffs on Mexico and Canada could have on their business.  Right now, there are 25 percent tariffs on all Mexican and Canadian imports.  One north Alabama farmer said he hopes the trade dispute between the U.S., Canada and Mexico is resolved soon.  Larry LouAllen is the owner of LouAllen Farms in Moulton. He said that he is concerned that the tariffs will do more harm than good to his farm and many others. LouAllen grows everything from strawberries, to peaches, and apples. The farm has even been in his family for over 100 years.  LouAllen said he uses a lot of peat moss that comes from Western Canada to grow his crops. He said an extended trade war would leave him with only two choices.  ‘We would either have to increase prices or sell at a loss,’ said LouAllen. ‘You can’t stay in business selling at a loss.’" [WAFF, 3/6/25]
