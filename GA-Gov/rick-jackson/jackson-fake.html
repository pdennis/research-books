Overview

While Rick Jackson promotes himself as a selfless philanthropist and gubernatorial candidate running to help Georgians, he faced backlash for using his wealth and power to do the opposite. 

Despite pitching himself as a philanthropist who would address rent and housing costs, Jackson bashed Georgians living in apartments and voiced a fear of children, all while boasting about his mega-mansion in a 2013 deposition. In 2012, Jackson sued the Forsyth County Board of Commissioners over zoning approval of apartments near his property. In his 2013 deposition, Jackson belittled people who lived in apartments, including children, and boasted about having the “nicest home in Georgia” and a lavish home in areas where people could not afford several acres of land. Jackson boasted having a dog “agility center” in his mega-mansion, which would have the closest proximity to the apartment complex. Jackson said having apartments adjacent to his mansion would be a "complete total disgrace and disrespect,” claimed "people that rent are not as responsible as people that own," and said the “type of people” that lived in apartments would affect the value of his mansion’s property and would increase security concerns for him. Jackson admitted to believing the stereotype that renters bring in “bigger security issue[s].” He said apartment children increased his security concerns, believing they would find a way to sneak into his property. Jackson complained that he needed to install a $500,000 security upgrade to prevent any potential crime associated with renters moving in near his property.

Jackson also touted the investment in a Texas data center and promoted the need for local input over data center development, yet faced backlash from Bowie County, Texas residents over his company’s development of a massive hyperscale data center campus. No Bowie Data Centers organized against the data center project linked to Jackson’s company and questioned his pledge for local input due to a confidentiality agreement between Bowie County Commissioners and Jackson’s company over the land sale.

No Bowie County Data Centers Organized Against Projects Linked To Jackson’s Company And Questioned Jackson's Pledge For Local Input Over Data Centers

HEADLINE: "Rick Jackson Says Communities Should Decide On Data Centers. But His Own Data Center Project In Texas Is Under Fire From Locals" [Courier Georgia, 9/11/26]

No Bowie County Data Centers Organized Against Projects Linked To Jackson’s Company, And A Leary, Texas Resident Questioned Jackson’s Claim He Supported Local Input Over Data Center Development. According to the Atlanta Journal-Constitution, "A group called No Bowie County Data Centers has organized against projects linked to Jackson’s company, raising concerns about the strain on the environment and the development process. Dozens of residents packed a county meeting last month before officials approved the sale of a 582-acre tract tied to the project. Danny Proctor, a Texas resident who lives in Leary near the data center site, told the AJC in an email that his attempts to reach Jackson over concerns about the project have gone unanswered. He questioned whether the Republican contender means it when he says ‘every community needs to have control of whether they have a data center or not — full stop,’ during his campaign speeches. ‘Those are strong words,’ Proctor wrote in an email to Jackson that was obtained by the AJC. ‘Now you have an opportunity to demonstrate whether they are also a standard you are willing to live by. Because, Mr. Jackson, a principle should not stop at the Georgia state line.’" [Atlanta Journal-Constitution, 9/15/26]

  • Jackson’s Op-Ed Highlighted His Pledge To Provide “Clear, Consistent Guidelines For Data Centers,” Noting There Needed To Be Local Support And Clear Local Benefit, And Data Centers Needed To “Pay Their Own Way And Meet Strong Environmental Standards.” According to Rick Jackson’s opinion in the Atlanta Journal-Constitution, "Politicians like to hedge and muddy the waters, especially on complicated and controversial issues like data centers. I’m not a politician, so I’ll give it to you straight. When I’m governor, Georgia will have clear, consistent guidelines for data centers. The short and simple version is this: Data centers must have local support, provide a clear local benefit, pay their own way and meet strong environmental standards. Data centers can bring investment and revenue to a community. But they can also place enormous demands on local land, roads, water and power. Those tradeoffs should never be decided behind closed doors or dictated by politicians hundreds of miles away. The people who live with the consequences must have a meaningful voice in the decision." [Rick Jackson Opinion – Atlanta Journal-Constitution, 8/27/26]

Bowie County Residents Were Upset Over A Confidentiality Agreement Between The County Commissioners And Jackson’s Company

Bowie County Residents Were Upset Over A Confidentiality Agreement Over The Land Sale For Data Center Development Between County Commissioners And Jackson’s Company. According to the Courier Georgia, "A separate document filed in Bowie County this August, a Waiver of Surface Rights, names Datahub Developers as the ‘Project Company’ planning to build on that land. That’s the same tract the county and economic development nonprofits voted in August to fully sell for data center development. A $12 million sale and a confidentiality clause That August sale is the one Proctor and his neighbors packed the Bowie County Courthouse to fight. Dozens of residents showed up to a special commissioners’ meeting to press officials on property values, pollution, water use, and the county’s approach to economic development before the sale was approved. County Judge Bobby Howell told the crowd that the three government entities had originally paid about $1.5 million for the land and were selling it for $12 million, a return he called ‘tremendous,’ separate from any revenue the data center itself might eventually generate. When Proctor, who attended the meeting, asked whether commissioners had signed a nondisclosure agreement covering the sale, Judge Howell confirmed they had, describing a confidentiality clause as standard practice in real estate contracts. ‘We signed a contract that had a confidentiality clause,’ Howell said. That pattern is exactly what strikes Proctor as irreconcilable with what Jackson has said publicly about data centers. In an op-ed for the Atlanta Journal-Constitution, Jackson wrote that decisions about data centers ‘should never be decided behind closed doors,’ and that the people who live with the consequences ‘must have a meaningful voice in the decision.’" [Courier Georgia, 9/11/26]
