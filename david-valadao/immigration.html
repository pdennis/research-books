ICE Had A History Of Detaining Immigrants With No Criminal Record In California 

74% Of ICE Arrests In July Were Immigrants With No Criminal Record

74% Of ICE Arrests In July Were Immigrants With No Criminal Record. According to AgAlert, "In July, the most recent month with data available, ICE arrests hit record highs both nationally and in California, due largely to the agency detaining more immigrants without criminal convictions, according to federal data analyzed by the Deportation Data Project, which is based at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA.  That month, 41,786 people nationwide were arrested and booked into immigration detention. About 74% of those detained did not have a criminal record—also a record high—while around 25% had been convicted of a nonviolent crime and less than 2% had a conviction for a violent crime." [AgAlert, 10/21/26]

ICE Overreach In California Raised Concern Among Farmers Of Disrupting Harvests Crucial To National Food Supply

Farms Across The Central Valley Expressed Concern That Immigration Raids Have Disrupted Harvest And Will Continue To Put Pressure On Farms In An Already Challenging Farm Economy 

Farms Across The Central Valley Report Immigration Raids Have Disrupted Harvest And The Ability To Fulfill Orders. According to Latin Times, "Immigration raids in California's Central Valley are disrupting agricultural production as farmworkers stay home amid fears of arrest, leaving growers struggling to harvest crops and fulfill orders, according to a new report by Ag Alert.  Kevin Herman, owner of The Specialty Crop Co. in Madera, said approximately half his 100 fig harvesters missed more than a week of work, reducing production and preventing the company from meeting orders from retailers across North America. ‘It's a little bit of a challenge when you have a sales order for five pallets of fruit and you only pick two or three,’ Herman told Ag Alert.  The labor shortage has also affected melon production. Fresno County grower Joe Del Bosque said several employees stayed home after immigration agents were spotted nearby, slowing operations that depend on approximately 20 workers trained to harvest, pack and transport cantaloupes. Joe Garcia, a farm labor contractor employing about 3,000 workers, said immigration arrests at supermarkets and roadsides, along with rumors of enforcement activity, have discouraged employees from reporting to work even when agents have not entered farms." [Latin Times, 10/23/26]

Growers Expressed Concern Immigration Enforcement Could Interfere With Citrus Harvest, Adding To Pressures From High Diesel Prices And Increased Farm Production Expenses. According to Latin Times, "Growers are concerned that continued enforcement could interfere with the approaching citrus harvest, adding to financial pressures already affecting the agricultural sector. Trump recently announced $8 billion in assistance for farmers as diesel prices remained approximately 75% higher than a year earlier. The Agriculture Department projected a 4.5% increase in farm production expenses in 2026." [Latin Times, 10/23/26]

California’s Agricultural Workforce Was Important To National Food Production, With More Than A Third Of Vegetables And Roughly Three-Quarters Of The Nation’s Fruit And Nuts Being Produced In California. According to Latin Times, "The potential consequences of a prolonged labor shortage extend beyond individual farms. California produces more than one-third of the nation's vegetables and roughly three-quarters of its fruits and nuts, making the availability of agricultural workers important to food production across the country."
